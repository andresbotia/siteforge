# DESIGN.md: Professional Services — Editorial

SiteForge-authored category reference. Generalized design PRINCIPLES only —
never business facts, never a fixed section schema, never a specific
business's palette or copy to reproduce. Reinterpret every principle below
for the actual business, category nuance, and verified facts of the job at
hand.

## Design intent

An established, trustworthy independent professional practice — not a
discount online service, not a faceless enterprise vendor. The page should
read like a firm that does careful, specific work for real clients, told
through restraint and precision rather than volume of claims.

## Emotional target

Calm competence. A visitor should feel the practice is careful, precise,
and unhurried — the opposite of a loud sales pitch. Confidence expressed
through clarity and craft, not superlatives.

## Intended audience

Local business owners, professionals, or individuals evaluating who to
trust with an ongoing, detail-sensitive relationship (legal, financial,
advisory, or similarly credential-driven work). They are reading closely,
not skimming for a coupon.

## Visual hierarchy

One dominant idea per screen. A single strong opening statement carries
more weight than a cluttered value-proposition list. Section headings are
real, structural landmarks — never decorative eyebrow-plus-heading pairs
repeated on every section — skimmable in isolation.

## Typography roles

- Display: an expressive serif with real character (a transitional or
  humanist serif with editorial weight), reserved for the hero statement
  and section headings — never for body copy.
- Body: a highly legible, restrained sans or serif at a comfortable reading
  size and generous line height. Longer passages should read like
  something a person would actually finish, not skim past.
- A third, optional role for structural labels (section numbers, category
  tags) — a monospace or small-caps treatment used as structure, never as
  decoration.

Two families plus one supporting label treatment is enough. Resist adding
a fourth.

## Color roles

A restrained palette: one warm or neutral ground, one deep ink for text and
structure, one considered accent used sparingly and with intention — never
as a decorative gradient. Color should mark meaning (a CTA, a section
boundary), not decorate. Avoid a bright, saturated "SaaS-blue" palette;
prefer tones that could plausibly belong to a real physical practice.

## Spacing / rhythm

Generous, deliberate whitespace — but purposeful, not merely empty. Section
rhythm should alternate visual grounds (a light section, a dark or tinted
section, back to light) so the page has a felt cadence when scrolled,
rather than one flat repeating background top to bottom.

## Composition philosophy

Editorial, not SaaS-dashboard. Real asymmetry where it serves the content —
a hero anchored to one side or the bottom of the screen with actual
content, not a centered headline floating over empty space. Content should
feel integrated into the layout, not boxed into interchangeable cards.

## Hero strategy

The opening screen should establish category identity, location context
(only if supplied), the core proposition, and a single clear next action —
without needing every fact up front. See the imagery-strategy note below
for the no-photography case.

## Section rhythm

A practice/approach section (how the work actually happens, in plain
language) tends to build more trust than a generic "why choose us" list. A
services section reads better as a clear, scannable set of real offerings
than as a repeated icon-card grid. Follow with only the trust and practical
sections the verified facts actually support — never pad with sections
that have nothing real to say.

## Imagery strategy

PHOTO_ABSENT must still feel intentional, not like a placeholder. A single,
well-considered original motif — an illustrative element tied to the
business's own name, craft, or locale, rendered as clean line art or a
restrained graphic device — can substitute honestly for photography
without ever implying it depicts real premises, staff, or work. Reuse that
one motif deliberately (for example, echoed faintly in a closing section)
rather than inventing a new decorative element per section. When real,
approved photography exists, prefer one excellent image used deliberately
over a gallery.

## CTA / conversion architecture

One dominant action (often a phone call for this category), stated plainly
and repeated at meaningful points (opening, closing, contact) — not buried
in a nav dropdown, not multiplied into competing CTAs. A secondary,
lower-emphasis action can support the primary one but should never compete
with it visually.

## Component treatment

Minimal card repetition. Where a list of items is genuinely needed (a
services list, a process outline), prefer an editorial list treatment —
numbered or labeled rows with real typographic hierarchy — over a grid of
identical bordered boxes. Reserve a card or box for something that
genuinely needs visual containment, not as a default building block
repeated throughout the page.

## Navigation behavior

Quiet and minimal — a wordmark, a handful of in-page anchors, one CTA.
Sticky or translucent navigation suits this category well on a page with
enough length to warrant it; it should never compete with the hero for
attention.

## Responsive principles

Mobile is a distinct design decision, not a shrunk desktop layout. A
structural device that works at desktop width (a side rail, a multi-column
grid) should collapse to a genuinely mobile-appropriate pattern (a stacked
list, an inline label) rather than simply narrowing. CTA text must never
risk an awkward two-line wrap at narrow widths.

## Accessibility expectations

Real semantic landmarks and heading hierarchy (one h1, meaningful h2s).
Sufficient contrast on every text/background pairing actually used, not
just the primary ones. A skip-to-content link. Descriptive alt text (or an
aria-label) on any illustrative graphic.

## Category-specific information priorities

Expertise and credibility, how the process works, services offered, how to
make contact, restrained and purposeful imagery. The Designer category
brief supplied separately for the specific job is the authoritative,
per-job version of this list — this DESIGN.md sets the visual language;
the brief sets which facts are actually available.

## Trust presentation

Trust here comes from precision of language and a clearly explained
process, not from claimed credentials, review counts, or superlatives that
were not supplied as verified facts. If verified trust facts exist (a
license, a rating, years in business), present them plainly and once —
never repeated for emphasis, never dressed up as a badge grid.

## Information-density rules

Say less, more precisely. A short, specific sentence beats a longer generic
one. Avoid marketing filler ("your trusted partner for all your needs") in
favor of concrete, honest scope statements grounded in the verified facts.

## Visual anti-patterns

No giant empty hero areas. No excessive pill badges. No generic
feature-card grids repeated down the page. No gratuitous glassmorphism. No
random or decorative gradients with no purpose. No excessive rounded
containers — restrained, mostly-square geometry suits this category's
seriousness better than a soft, playful radius everywhere.

## Adaptation guidance

Reinterpret every principle above for the specific business's own
personality, category nuance, and available facts — this is a starting
design language, not a template to instantiate literally. Two different
professional-services businesses drawing on this same reference should not
look identical to each other.
